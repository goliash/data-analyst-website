Full disclosure

Last updated October 15, 2026. What this site collects, what it doesn’t, and the things most sites bury. If anything here is unclear, tell me and I’ll fix the page.

The short version. There are no ads, and I never sell or share your data. I use Google Analytics, through Google Tag Manager, to count visits, so I can see which pages people read. You can switch it off below, along with motion. Apart from Google’s analytics and Tag Manager code, nothing on this site loads from anyone else’s server. Not even fonts. Google Analytics sets its own cookies. My cookies only remember your settings, and only if you change one. The count sheet, the KPI lab and the Data analyst page run in your browser and send nothing. If you open your own file there, it is never uploaded or stored. If you use the contact form, it collects some technical details along with your message. That part is worth reading.

1. What the contact form collects

When you press send, your name, email, company (if you give one) and message go to me. Two sets of details go along with them. Nothing is collected when the page just loads. Most contact forms collect these quietly. I’d rather list them.

Read from your connection (dependable)

My host reports these about the message itself. They can’t be faked from your end, and they are never read out of what you typed:

WhatWhy it’s there
IP addressShows roughly where a message came from, and lets me block a source if spam floods in.
City, state, country, ZIP areaA rough guess from the IP, not your device’s location. It’s often off by quite a bit.
TimezoneSo I don’t reply at three in the morning your time.
Internet providerWhich provider or company network the message came through.
Data centerWhich of my host’s data centers handled it. Only useful for fixing problems.
TimestampWhen it arrived, in UTC.

Reported by your browser (take with salt)

Your browser offers these on its own. You can change or turn off any of them, and nothing breaks if you do:

WhatWhy it’s there
Device type, platform, browserIf you tell me something looks broken, this saves a round of “what are you using?”
Screen and window sizeSame reason. Most “it looks wrong” reports get solved by this one line.
Timezone and UTC offsetDouble-checks the one above.
LanguagesSo I reply in one you read.
CPU cores, memory, touch supportContext if you’re telling me something is slow.
Referring pageHow you found the site. Blank if you typed the address.
User agent stringThe long version of the browser line.

Being straight with you

Put together, that’s a fairly clear picture of your computer. I collect it because it helps me answer faster, and it helps me tell a real person from the steady drip of spam. I won’t pretend it’s nothing.

Your message arrives as an email in my inbox. A copy is also saved as one row in a private Google Sheet owned by the same account, so messages are easy to find later. I don’t build a profile from any of it, and I don’t sell it or share it. Ask me to delete your message and I will, and I’ll tell you when it’s done.

In plain terms: your email address is never sold, rented, shared with advertisers or disclosed to anyone else for their own purposes. You can unsubscribe at any time, using the link in every email I send or the standing unsubscribe page, and you can ask me to delete your data at any time. The unsubscribe page saves your email address, the time, your reason if you give one, and whether you asked me to delete your messages. It doesn’t save your IP address or device details.

If you’d rather not send any of that, email me directly. The address is on the front page, next to the form. A regular email carries only what your own mail app sends, which is a lot less.

2. Cookies, storage and Google Analytics

Motion and animation

The carousel, the particles, counting numbers and the opening story. On for everyone. If movement on screen bothers you, switch it off here and it stays off on every page.

Google Analytics and Tag Manager

Counts visits so I can see which pages people read. Google Tag Manager is the Google tool that holds these tags. On unless you switch it off. Off means neither is loaded, and Google’s cookies are deleted.

  • Light or dark mode. Click the button at the top and your choice is saved in a cookie that lasts a year. Never touch the button and nothing is saved.
  • Motion. Switch it off above and a cookie (motion=off) remembers that for a year. Switch it back on and the cookie is deleted. Leave it alone and nothing is saved.
  • Google Analytics. Google’s code, loaded through Google Tag Manager (container GTM-XXXXXXX), sets two cookies, _ga and _ga_XXXXXXXXXX, that last up to two years. They let Google tell a new visit from a return visit. Switch analytics off above and a cookie (analytics=off) remembers that for a year.
  • The opening animation. For this visit only, your browser remembers that you already saw it, so it doesn’t play again on every page. That uses session storage. It isn’t a cookie, it never leaves your computer, and it’s gone when you close the tab.

That’s the whole list. No advertising, no other tracking pixels, no session recording.

What Google Analytics sees

Which pages you open and for how long, roughly where you are (the city, which Google works out from your connection; Google says it doesn’t keep the address itself), your device and browser, and the page that sent you here. Google also notices clicks on links that leave this site, file downloads, scrolling to the bottom of a page, and when a form is started or sent. It never sees what you type in a form or the files you open on the Data analyst page.

I see totals and trends, not you. I don’t use it for ads, and I don’t combine it with anything else. Google handles the data under its own privacy policy. If your browser sends Global Privacy Control, analytics stays off on its own.

3. The count sheet and the KPI lab

Both run entirely in your browser. The count sheet uses made-up items and numbers, not data from any company I have worked for. The KPI lab starts with sample numbers too. Nothing you type in it is sent anywhere. If you choose File > Save, your numbers are kept in your browser’s own storage on your device (not a cookie, never sent) until you choose Edit > Delete saved numbers. The desktop version keeps them on your computer the same way and never goes online.

4. The Data analyst page and your own data

The villager dashboard uses public fan data about the game Animal Crossing: New Horizons: the villager list and the Nook Market wish list from a Kaggle notebook, a fan tier list, and a second copy of the villagers from VillagerDB, shared by the TidyTuesday project. None of it is about a real person. The villager pictures are the game’s own icons and photos, which belong to Nintendo. They come from the fan-made ACNH API project and are stored on this site, so loading them doesn’t contact anyone else.

I do not store your data. When you open a file, paste rows or drop a CSV into “Explore your own data,” your own browser reads it and draws the charts. The file is never uploaded, not to me, not to my host and not to anyone else. It isn’t saved in a cookie or in your browser’s storage either. Nothing about it is logged. Reload or close the page, or press “Forget this data,” and it’s gone. The only thing that can leave the page is a CSV of a chart that you choose to download, and that goes to your own downloads folder. Google Analytics, if it’s on, records only that you visited the page, never your data.
