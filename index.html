Picture the interview

The job: data analyst at a plant or a warehouse in Northeast PA. Here are six questions interviewers often ask, with my real answers. As you scroll, the pictures follow along.

  1. Picture it

    You have my résumé. We sit down. Each answer below is mine, and every detail in the pictures comes from my own work.

  2. Question 1 of 6

    “Walk me through your background.”

    I started in 2008 at Crestline, checking and fixing client data in Access and SQL Server, two Microsoft programs that store and sort large amounts of data. When Harbor Benefits bought Crestline, I was promoted to senior systems analyst. In 2018 I joined Summit Modular Homes as a Data Analyst / IT Specialist, building spreadsheets, supporting plant IT, and implementing data applications to handle large volumes of modular manufacturing data. Since 2021 I’ve run reporting at Keystone Saddlery Supply, a distributor of horse-riding (equestrian) supplies. From 2022 to early 2026, I was also the IT director at Northgate Furniture Co., a furniture plant, in charge of its computers and network. I kept my Keystone Saddlery Supply job part-time during those years.

  3. Question 2 of 6

    “Tell me about a time the numbers didn’t match.”

    At Keystone Saddlery Supply, we count what’s really on the shelves and compare it with what Sage, our business software, says should be there. When a bin (one spot on a shelf) doesn’t match, I don’t just type in the new number. I find out why. Once, an order had shipped, but nobody marked it as shipped in Sage, so Sage still thought it was on the shelf. Another time, the item was sitting in the wrong bin. Each time, I fixed what caused the mismatch. Then the count matched for real.

  4. Question 3 of 6

    “How do you keep reports going out?”

    I keep Knowledge Sync running. It’s a program that pulls numbers out of Sage and emails reports on a schedule, more than 10 every day: open orders, backorders (orders waiting for stock to come in), shipping and sales. People get them without having to ask, and nobody has to rebuild the same report by hand. Our purchasing team uses my reports to see what’s selling, plan ahead, and order equestrian supplies in time to fill customer needs.

  5. Question 4 of 6

    “Have you worked in a plant?”

    Yes. At Northgate Furniture Co., a furniture plant in Millbrook, I ran JobBOSS, the plant’s main business system (its ERP), which tracked every job from order to shipping. I built reports on orders and shipping for three groups: the plant leaders, the supervisors, and the purchasing team, the people who order the materials. Before that, at Summit Modular Homes, I fixed computer problems right on the factory floor.

  6. Question 5 of 6

    “What if we use a tool you haven’t used?”

    I’ll tell you, and then I’ll learn it. Right now I’m learning Power BI, Microsoft’s tool for charts and dashboards. So far my reports have been in Crystal Reports, Excel and Access, pulling data from SQL Server databases and ERP systems. The skills carry over to any tool: start with clean data, measure what matters, and build every report with the reader in mind.

  7. Question 6 of 6

    “Why should we hire you?”

    I make sure the numbers are right before anyone acts on them. I check where each number came from, find the cause when one is wrong, and explain it in plain words. That protects your profit. When the inventory count is right, you don’t run out of what customers want, and you don’t tie up cash in stock nobody needs. Customers get their orders, and they keep coming back.

    The rest of this interview is yours.

What I bring to an operations team

  • Reports people use

    I build and keep up automated reports in Crystal Reports, Excel and Access. More than 10 go out every day on their own, so nobody waits for someone to make one by hand. The purchasing team uses them to decide what to order and when.

KPI lab

Each one shows the formula, explains it in plain words, and says how I would read the result. It all runs on your own device and nothing you type is sent anywhere. KPI Lab — Inventory accuracy

Inventory

Of the bins you counted, how many matched the system?

With JavaScript off, here is one worked example: 492 of 500 counted bins matched the system, so inventory accuracy is 98.4%.

Inventory accuracy

98.4%

492 matched ÷ 500 counted

0%Target 98%100%

    8 of 500 bins were off. Recount those first. Then look for a pattern by item, aisle or shift so it doesn’t repeat.

    In plain words

    Inventory accuracy tells you how often the computer is right about what’s on the shelf. Count a set of bins, then check each count against the system. A bin only counts as a match when the two agree exactly.

    The formula

    Inventory accuracy = bins that matched ÷ bins counted × 100

    Why it matters

    If the computer says you have 10 and the shelf has 4, you’ll promise orders you can’t ship. Every other report is only as good as this number.

    Plain-English dictionary

    Short forms and shop talk from this page, in plain words. Anywhere on the page, tap a highlighted word to see what it means in a bubble.

    Active Directory
    The list at work that says who can log in and what each person is allowed to open.
    API
    A doorway one computer program uses to ask another program for something and get an answer back.
    B2B web portal
    A website for other businesses, not shoppers. They log in to see prices and place orders.
    Backorder
    An order that has to wait because the item is out of stock. It ships when new stock comes in.
    Barcode scanner
    A handheld reader that beeps when it reads the striped label on a box, so nobody has to type the item number.
    Bin
    One small spot on a shelf with its own address, like A-04-02. It’s how a warehouse knows where each thing lives.
    CAD
    Computer-aided design: drawing things, like the parts of a house, on a computer instead of on paper.
    Census data
    In benefits work, a list of a company’s workers: names, birthdays, pay and so on.
    Cleanse (data)
    Fix the typos, copies and blanks in data so people can trust it.
    Cloudflare
    A company with computers all over the world that show websites fast. This site lives there for free.
    CRM
    Customer relationship management: a program that remembers every customer, what they bought, and when someone last called them.
    Crystal Reports
    A program that turns rows of data into neat reports you can print or email.
    Cycle count
    Counting a few bins every day, instead of shutting down to count the whole warehouse once a year.
    Days of supply
    How many days your stock will last if sales keep the same pace.
    Distributor
    A company that buys lots of goods from the people who make them, then sells them to stores and other businesses.
    Downtime
    Time a machine was supposed to be running, but wasn’t.
    EOQ
    Economic order quantity: the order size that keeps the cost of ordering plus the cost of storing as low as it can be.
    ERP
    Enterprise resource planning: the main computer system a company runs on. It keeps orders, stock, jobs, shipping and money in one place. JobBOSS and Sage are both ERPs.
    Fill rate
    Out of everything customers ordered, how much you actually sent them.
    HR
    Human resources: the team that hires people and takes care of pay and benefits.
    Inventory accuracy
    How often the computer’s count matches what’s really on the shelf.
    Inventory turns
    How many times a year you sell all your stock and buy it again.
    IT
    Information technology: computers, networks and software, and the people who keep them working.
    JobBOSS
    An ERP made for factories and job shops. It follows each job from the order to the shipping dock.
    Knowledge Sync
    A program that watches business data and emails reports on a schedule, all by itself.
    KPI
    Key performance indicator: one number that tells you how something is going, like a grade on a report card.
    Lead time
    How long you wait between ordering something and having it on your shelf.
    Linux
    A free operating system, like Windows, that runs most of the world’s servers.
    LLM
    Large language model: the kind of AI that reads and writes words, like ChatGPT or Claude.
    LTL
    Less than truckload: a shipment too big for a parcel service but too small to fill a truck, so it shares one with other shipments.
    Modular construction
    Building parts of a house in a factory, then trucking them to the lot to put together.
    NAS
    Network-attached storage: a box of hard drives on the office network that keeps shared files and backups.
    OEE
    Overall equipment effectiveness: how much of a machine’s planned time made good parts at full speed.
    Perfect order
    An order with nothing wrong: on time, complete, not damaged, and billed right.
    Pivot table
    An Excel tool that adds up thousands of rows in seconds, like total sales for each month.
    Power BI
    Microsoft’s tool for making charts and dashboards from data.
    Proxmox
    Free software that lets one computer act like several smaller computers.
    Purchasing team
    The people who decide what to buy, how much, and when, so the shelves never run empty.
    Reconcile
    Compare two lists that should match, like the shelf count and the computer, then fix the reason they don’t.
    Reorder point
    The stock level that tells you: time to order more.
    Safety stock
    A few extra on the shelf, just in case sales jump or a truck is late.
    Sage
    Business software that keeps track of stock, orders, customers and money.
    Scrap
    Things you made that can’t be sold or fixed, so they get thrown out.
    SQL
    Structured query language: the words you use to ask a database a question, like “show me every late order.” SQL Server is Microsoft’s database.
    Starlink
    Internet that comes from satellites. At the plant it was the backup if the regular line went down.
    Tailscale
    A tool that builds a private, locked network (a VPN) between your own devices.
    Takt time
    The beat of a factory line: how often one finished thing has to come off the end to keep up with orders.
    Ticketing system
    A to-do list for help requests. Each problem gets a number, so nothing gets forgotten.
    Ubiquiti and Cisco
    Brands of network gear: the boxes that connect a building’s computers to each other and to the internet.
    UPS WorldShip and FedEx Ship Manager
    Shipping programs that print box labels and send out tracking numbers.
    VBA
    Visual Basic for Applications: a small programming language inside Excel and Access that makes them do boring jobs by themselves.
    VLOOKUP and XLOOKUP
    Excel tools that find something in one list and bring back the match, like looking up a price by part number.
    VPN
    Virtual private network: a private, locked tunnel over the internet, so only your own devices can get in.
